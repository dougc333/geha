## Trends in the Health Workforce Supply in the Rural U.S.

## KEY FINDINGS

## Trends in the Supply of Rural Health Workforce

-  Nationally, the supply per 100,000 population of primary care physicians (including family physicians, general internal medicine physicians, and pediatricians) overall was lower in rural counties than in urban counties (55.6 vs. 93.6, respectively).
-  There were fewer general internal medicine physicians and pediatricians in rural compared to urban counties, but there was a similar number of family physicians per 100,000 population in rural and urban counties (35.0 vs. 33.9, respectively).
-  There was substantial growth in rural nurse practitioner and physician assistant supply over the past 20 years with increases in the per capita supply more than quadrupling and tripling, respectively.
-  Nationally, the supply of behavioral health clinicians, including psychiatrists, psychologists, psychiatric nurse practitioners, social workers, and counselors, was significantly lower in rural counties compared to urban counties. The number of behavioral health clinicians per 100,000 population in rural counties ranged from 27% (psychiatrists) to 70% (psychiatric nurse practitioners) of urban areas.
-  The number of obstetricians per 100,000 women of childbearing age in rural counties in 2019 was less than half the number in urban counties (30.3 vs. 62.9, respectively).
-  More than half of all U.S. rural counties lacked an obstetrician (57.4%), and more than three-quarters lacked an advanced practice midwife (75.1%) or midwife (87.4%).
-  The supply of general surgeons per 100,000 population in rural areas in 2019 was about half of that in urban areas (6.7 vs. 13.1, respectively).
-  The supply of general surgeons per 100,000 population decreased in both rural (-11.8%) and urban (-8.4%) areas from 1995 to 2019.
- Nationally, the ratio of dentists per 100,000 population in rural areas (32.1) was about 55% that of urban areas (59.4).
-  From 1995 to 2019, the national rural ratio of dentists per 100,000 population decreased by 7.0% while the urban ratio increased by 2.6%.

## Rural Health Workforce Data Sources

-    The Area Health Resources File (AHRF), published annually by the Bureau of Health Workforce (BHW) within the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), is a key source of current and historic county-level data for physicians and dentists, but it does not include data on some health care professions, particularly non-physician behavioral health and obstetrical care clinicians, or dental care professionals other than dentists.
-  The National Plan and Provider Enumeration System National Provider Identifier (NPPES NPI) data, maintained by the Centers for Medicare &amp; Medicaid Services, contains information on many types of professions and is a key source of non-physician data.
-  Health care professionals who do not typically directly bill for their services are not required to obtain an NPI number or may bill using a group NPI number, so the counts of some clinicians may not be reflected accurately in the NPI registry.
- Historic NPI data are not available for download, which limits the ability to conduct trend analyses.
-  Estimates of the supply of some health care professions using the Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) data published by the U.S. Department of Labor were significantly lower than other established data sources such as the AHRF. These inconsistencies raise concerns about the use of the OEWS data for health workforce supply research.
-  National profession-specific databases exist but are frequently cost prohibitive for researchers to access.

## BACKGROUND

The U.S. health care system requires an adequate workforce to provide care for the population, yet rural communities often face threats to health workforce supply. These threats include challenges recruiting and retaining health professionals, an aging rural workforce, and the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, which has led to burnout, early retirement, and deaths of rural health care workers. 1-4

The scope of the challenge is vast: Over 60 percent of the primary care, mental health, and dental professional shortage areas were in rural areas as of December 2023. 5  Prior research by the WWAMI Rural Health Research Center, and others, reports that the supply of many rural health care professionals, as measured in provider-to-population ratios, is lower than in urban areas. Among these professionals are general internists, pediatricians, urologists, general surgeons, nurse practitioners, registered nurses, physician assistants, pharmacists, dentists, obstetricians, midwives, psychiatrists, psychologists, psychiatric nurse practitioners, optometrists, and emergency responders. 6-17  Family physicians and licensed practical nurses (LPNs) are two bright spots in that their concentration per capita is higher in rural than in urban areas. 6,15  However, rural areas have seen a greater decline over time in the density of primary care clinicians per capita than urban areas. 18  Because of the lower supply of health care clinicians in rural areas, rural residents drive longer distances to obtain care and have fewer annual visits with health care clinicians than urban residents. 19,20

In the past decade, the number of younger physicians entering the rural workforce has been declining, leading to aging of the rural physician workforce overall. 3  In 2017, over half of rural physicians were at least 50 years old, and over a quarter were at least 60 years old. As these trends continue, the disparity of rural vs. urban physician supply is expected to widen by 2030. 3  A similar trend of fewer younger dentists choosing rural practice has also been observed. 21  The number of advanced practice professionals, such as nurse practitioners and physician assistants, has been growing nationally to potentially meet the need created by this gap, but the number of these clinicians remains lower in rural  compared to urban communities. 6,22

<!-- image -->

Tracking trends in the rural health care workforce is critical for identifying potential gaps and developing targeted solutions to ensure an optimal supply and mix of well-prepared, culturally responsive health professionals to care for rural populations. However, the availability of information to characterize rural health care workforce supply and distribution is inconsistent across the various types of health professions. 23  This report examines the availability of current and historical workforce data and identifies data gaps for a variety of health care professionals in primary care, behavioral health, maternal health, oral health, and other services important to rural communities. Where data allow, we provide estimates of trends in the supply and distribution of multiple health professionals in the rural vs. urban U.S. nationally and regionally.

## Health Workforce Data Sources and Availability

We explored publicly available health care workforce data that allowed us to make urban and rural specific estimates of supply. We investigated the Area Health Resources Files (AHRF) from the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System National Provider Identifier (NPPES NPI) from the Centers for Medicare &amp; Medicaid Services (CMS), the Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the American Community Survey (ACS), and the National Sample Survey of Registered Nurses and Nurse Practitioners (NSSRN/NSSNP). The AHRF, NPI, OEWS, NSSRN/NSSNP, and ACS are all publicly available, no-cost workforce data sources, but they differ in the geography, types of professions included, and years of data available (see summary in Table 1). We also explored data requiring purchase, including the American Medical Association (AMA) Physician Masterfile and the American Dental Association (ADA) Dentist Masterfile.

## Area Health Resources Files (AHRF)

The Bureau of Health Workforce (BHW) within the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) publishes the county-level AHRF annually. 24  The AHRF compiles data from 50 different data sources on health care professional counts and demographics, health care facilities, hospital utilization and expenditures, and population characteristics. The health care workforce data sources used in the AHRF include the American Medical Association (AMA) Physician Masterfile, the American Dental Association (ADA) Masterfile, and the NPI data from CMS (other professions). One main advantage of the AHRF is that it contains a variety of health care workforce data that would otherwise be difficult for researchers to obtain individually, especially the AMA and ADA Masterfiles. The AHRF also includes population counts and rural-urban category variables, such as the U.S. Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service's Urban Influence Codes (UIC) and RuralUrban Continuum Codes (RUCC) that can be useful for analyses. The AHRF includes some historical workforce data, although the availability of these data varies by health care profession. A disadvantage of the AHRF is that it does not have data on some health care professions, particularly non-physician behavioral health and obstetrical care clinicians, and dental care professionals other than dentists.

## National Provider Identification (NPI)

As a requirement of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), all health care professionals who bill insurance electronically or through a clearinghouse for their services are required to have an NPI number. 25  Health care professionals who do not bill for services have the option to apply for an NPI number. To obtain an NPI number, clinicians must provide the NPI registry with their name, business address, age, sex, occupation taxonomy code, and license information. CMS publishes a downloadable file of the full NPI registry every month. 26

<!-- image -->

While the NPI registry was designed for administrative purposes, it can be used for workforce research. Unlike other data sources limited to specific professions, the NPI registry includes all types of clinicians and is a critical source of non-physician workforce data. With the clinician's business address available in the file, researchers can use the ZIP code and U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)-U.S. Postal Service ZIP Code Crosswalk files to classify their practice location by county, census tract, and other rural-urban classifications. 27

However, there are limitations to using the NPI data in research. While health care professionals are encouraged to update their information in the registry, they are not required to do so. Therefore, the accuracy of the information in the files is not known, including the percentage of clinicians listed who are currently professionally active or if their practice location is current. Clinicians also self-select their profession and specialty taxonomy codes, which can cause inconsistencies in the taxonomy codes used. Vanderlaan and Jefferson found that 20 percent of midwives in the NPI registry had the wrong taxonomy selected when compared to licensure data from the American Midwifery Certification Board. 28  CMS also does not have historical NPI registry data available for download, limiting trend analysis. Additionally, because some professionals who do not typically directly bill for their services are not required to obtain an NPI number, the counts of some advanced practice professions may not be reflected accurately in the registry. One study of clinical nurse specialists (CNS) found that only 11 percent of the estimated CNS workforce were described in the NPI registry. 29  Some professionals may opt to bill using a group NPI rather than obtaining an individual NPI, and those individuals may not be included in the counts of individual professionals.

## Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS)

The BLS with the U.S. Department of Labor annually publishes estimates of the number of workers and wages of full- and parttime workers for over 800 occupations in the 50 U.S. states, the District of Columbia, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands. 30  More than 60 health care occupations are included in the OEWS estimates including physicians overall, primary care physicians, obstetricians, psychiatrists, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, dentists, surgeons, advanced practice midwives (referred to as 'nurse midwives' in OEWS), social workers, and counselors. Some limited physician and dentist specialty data are available, but not all specialties are available. 31  The employment estimates are counts of full- and parttime workers paid wages or salaries, but they do not include self-employed workers, business owners, partners in a firm, or unpaid volunteers. The OEWS include estimates of health care occupational employment in rural (nonmetropolitan) vs. urban (metropolitan) by state. The BLS uses a definition of metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas based on metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs). 32  However, they do not provide estimates at any smaller levels of geography that would allow for analyses of more detailed rural categories (eg, micropolitan, noncore). The years of available metropolitan and nonmetropolitan data for specific health care professions vary. Not all metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas in the OEWS have estimates for all occupations. 33

The BLS generates annual estimates in the OEWS by surveying approximately 1.2 million nonfarm establishments over three years. BLS defines an 'establishment' as a discrete physical location where economic activity occurs, such as a physician's office, clinic, or hospital. The sample size for generating the employment estimates varies by occupation, depending on the prevalence of the occupation in the sampled establishments. 34  We compared estimates of dentists, psychiatrists, and obstetricians in the OEWS metro/nonmetro data to the counts available in the AHRF (Table 2). For all three professions and in both metro and nonmetro areas, the OEWS had significantly lower estimates compared to the AHRF in the U.S. overall and in all Census Divisions. In the nonmetro areas, the OEWS estimates of psychiatrists and obstetricians were missing data in many Census Divisions, further lowering estimates.

## American Community Survey (ACS)

The ACS is an ongoing nationwide survey conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau. The Census Bureau surveys approximately 3.5 million households every year to generate estimates on a broad range of population, social, economic, housing, and demographic variables between each decennial census, including estimates of various health care occupations. The ACS uses a coding scheme based on the Standard Occupation Classification (SOC) to label occupations. 35  In this scheme, most health care professions are in the 'Health Care Practitioners and Technical Occupations' (eg, dentists, physician assistants, nurse practitioners, physicians, etc), 'Health Care Support Occupations' (eg, home health aides, nursing assistants, dental assistants, etc), and 'Community and Social Services Occupations' (eg, counselors, therapists, social workers, etc) classifications. The only physician classifications available in the ACS are emergency medicine physicians, surgeons, and all other physicians.

The ACS estimates are available for a wide range of geographic units, including at the state, county, census tract, and block group levels. However, there is some limited availability of ACS data files in geographies with lower populations, including many rural areas. The three main types of ACS files are: 1-year files, 1-year supplemental estimates, and 5-year files. The 1-year files and 1-year supplemental estimates combine data collected over a 12-month period to generate estimates that apply to that year. The 1-year files contain data only for geographic areas that have a population of at least 65,000 people, and the 1-year supplemental estimates contain data only for geographic areas with at least 20,000 population. 35  In order to generate estimates for populations smaller than 20,000, the Census combines five years of data into a single estimate in the 5-year files. The 5-year files are the only ACS data files that contain estimates for all geographic areas (including counties, census tracts, census blocks, etc) in the U.S., regardless of population size. 35,36  The estimates for rural areas also tend to have larger margins of error than the estimates for urban areas because they are based on smaller samples. 36

## National Sample Survey of Registered Nurses (NSSRN) &amp; National Sample Survey of Nurse Practitioners (NSSNP)

The NSSRN and NSSNP are two publicly available surveys conducted approximately every four years by the U.S. Census Bureau and HRSA. The surveys are designed to describe the characteristics and distribution of the registered nurse and nurse practitioner workforce respectively, in the 50 U.S. states and the District of Columbia. In 2018, the NSSRN and NSSNP were conducted together for the first time, but prior to that, these surveys were conducted in different years. The NSSRN collects data on registered nurses' demographics, employment (including geographic location, employment setting, patient populations, and clinical specialty), license, education (including degree level, location of degree program, date of graduation), earnings, and hours worked. The NSSNP includes additional questions about the nurse practitioners' areas of certification, characteristics of their patient population, billing and insurance, perceptions/satisfaction with their work, and the presence of physicians at their work. 37

The NSSRN and NSSNP data are available at the state and county levels for most years that the data were collected. County Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) codes are available in the county-level NSSRN and NSSNP data that makes linkages to other county data and rural-classifications possible. There are historic state and county level data for the NSSRN available for download every four years between 1977 and 2008. However, only the 2012 NSSNP is publicly available at the county level. The most recent year of data collected for the NSSRN and NSSNP, 2018, only has state level data publicly available. 38  Additional restricted data from the 2018 NSSRN may be accessible through an application process with the Research Data Center at the U.S. Census Bureau. 39

## American Medical Association (AMA) Physician Masterfile

The AMA Physician Masterfile, now referred to as the AMA Physician Professional Data™, contains annually updated information from a variety of sources on physicians in the U.S., Puerto Rico, U.S. Virgin Islands, and some Pacific island territories. 40,41  Data elements in the Masterfile include physician age, major professional activity (type of practice in present employment, eg, office-based practice, hospital staff, administration, etc), specialty, medical school attended, year of graduation, and licensure information. 42  The Masterfile also contains the ZIP code of each physician's office address, which allows classification of their location by several rural-urban designations. The AMA Masterfile is not publicly available but can be purchased from AMA licensed vendors. 43  Historic physician data from the AMA Masterfile are available for years 1996 to 2021 only from one of the AMA licensed vendors, Medical Marketing Service, Inc. 44

## American Dental Association (ADA) Masterfile

Similar to the AMA Masterfile, the ADA Masterfile is an up-to-date record of all dentists in the 50 U.S. states and District of Columbia, maintained by the American Dental Association. The Masterfile includes demographic and practice information on dentists, including date of birth, sex, race, specialty, type of professional activity (private practice, hospital staff dentist, dental school faculty, etc), year and school of dental school graduation, and business address including ZIP code. 45  The ADA Masterfile is updated routinely from a variety of sources, including various censuses and surveys of dentists conducted by the ADA and state licensure databases. 46  The ADA Masterfile is not publicly available, but current and historic data may be purchased from the ADA.

## Data Availability and Gaps

The AHRF and NPI are valuable publicly available data sources for nationwide rural health care workforce research. Both sources allow for analyses by detailed rural-urban categories. Yet both sources have distinct gaps in the information available to researchers. The AHRF provides the greatest availability of historical data, including at least a decade of past years' data on most professions in the current files available for download. However, the AHRF only includes comprehensive specialty and professional activity data on physicians and dentists. Specialty data on some health care professions, including nurse practitioners, physician assistants, and non-physician behavioral health and obstetrical care clinicians are not available in the AHRF.

The NPI data includes specialty information on all health care professionals who bill individually for insurance, but the NPI historical data are not available for download. Researchers who regularly download the current NPI files may have historical NPI data over time, but these data are not publicly available for new researchers.

The ACS is a publicly available workforce data source that allows users to generate estimates of various health care occupations. The ACS has a wider variety of occupations than the AHRF, and there are more historical years of data available to download than the NPI data. The ACS can be used to generate estimates for geographies as small as the Census Block level but estimates for rural areas with small populations less than 20,000 people are only available in the 5-year ACS files.

OEWS health workforce estimates differ substantially from the counts available in the AHRF and NPI. These inconsistencies raise concerns about the use of the OEWS data for health workforce supply research (Table 2).

Other single profession and single state workforce data sources are available but can be more challenging to obtain. The NSSRN/NSSNP provides a detailed look at registered nurse and nurse practitioner workforce at the county-level, but to access county-level data from 2012 to present, researchers must apply for restricted data access with the Census Bureau. Researchers can request access to the AMA and ADA Masterfiles from AMA vendors and the ADA for a fee. Some states collect data on their health care workforce, but the availability and comparability of data varies by state and profession. 47

Expanding the availability of health care workforce data would improve researchers' ability to monitor changes to the rural health care workforce and assist policy makers in developing realistic solutions to address rural workforce disparities.

## Trend Analyses Methods

We used the AHRF (2003-2021, see Table 3 for the AHRF release used for each year of data) and the NPI NPPES data (20142021) to identify health professionals. We used the 2003 and 2013 Urban Influence Codes (UIC) from the U.S. Department of Agriculture, Economic Research Service (ERS) to classify the level of rurality of counties and health professionals, and the 2010-2020 County Population Data from the U.S. Census Bureau.

We selected the data sources based on the completeness of data availability for each profession. We used the following data sources for each health care profession:

-    AHRF - Primary care physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, psychiatrists, obstetricians, dentists, and general surgeons.
-    NPI - Psychologists, psychiatric nurse practitioners, social workers, counselors, advanced practice midwives, and midwives.

We calculated counts for each type of clinician in each county. We categorized all U.S. counties into one of five geographic categories using the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Economic Research Service Urban Influence Codes (UIC) from 2003 and 2013:

-   Urban: Metropolitan counties with an urban core with a population of at least 50,000 residents (UIC 1-2).
-   Adjacent to metro: Counties next to a metropolitan county whose largest town has up to 49,999 residents (UIC 3-7).
-     Micro nonadjacent to metro: Micropolitan counties not next to a metropolitan county whose largest town has 10,00049,999 residents (UIC 8).
-    Small and remote adjacent to micro: Counties next to a micropolitan county whose largest town has less than 10,000 residents (UIC 9-10).
-    Small and remote nonadjacent to metro or micro: Counties not next to a metropolitan or micropolitan county whose largest town has less than 10,000 residents (UIC 11-12).

<!-- image -->

We combined the above categories except urban to create an overall rural category with the following definition: All rural/ nonmetropolitan - counties without an urban core population of at least 50,000 residents (UIC 3-12).

We used the 2003 UICs to classify counties for data years from 2012 and earlier, and the 2013 UICs for classifying counties in data years 2013 and later. We used Census population data available in the AHRF to calculate clinicians per 100,000 population ratios at the county and Census Division levels. We used population estimates that were contemporaneous with workforce supply estimates (eg, 1995 population estimates with 1995 psychiatrist supply). For obstetrical care professions, we obtained populations of women of childbearing age (ages 15-49) from the county characteristic data from the U.S. Census Bureau to calculate ratios of clinicians per 100,000 women of childbearing age. We conducted analyses using SAS 9.4. The University of Washington Institutional Review Board determined that this research was exempt from human subjects review.

## RESULTS

The full data briefs for each profession are included at the end of this report. Below we summarize the findings from these briefs grouped by clinical specialty.

## Physicians

The per capita supply of physicians in rural counties was about 40% of the supply in urban counties in 2019.  From 1960 to 2019, the supply increased more in urban counties (126.4%) than in rural counties (79.9%). More rural counties did not have a physician in 2019 (6.2%) compared to urban counties (1.2%). There was significant variation between different types of rural places, micropolitan counties that were not adjacent to a metropolitan county had the highest supply per 100,000 population (186.6), more than twice the supply in small and remote counties adjacent to micropolitan counties (75.0).

## Primary Care Clinicians

Nationally from 1995 to 2019, the per capita supply of primary care clinicians was notably lower in rural areas compared to urban areas. Most recently, the per capita rural supply of primary care physicians, nurse practitioners, and physician assistants ranged from 60% to 80% of the urban supply. There has been substantial growth in both the rural nurse practitioner and physician assistant supply over the past 20 years with the per capita supply of both more than tripling, (416% and 312%, respectively). Variation in the per capita supply of primary care clinicians was evident among types of rural counties. The rural supply per 100,000 population for primary care physicians and physician assistants was highest in micropolitan counties not adjacent to a metropolitan county (72.4 and 40.7, respectively) and lowest in small and remote rural counties adjacent to a micropolitan county (46.2 and 27.1, respectively). The rural nurse practitioners supply was greatest in micropolitan counties not adjacent to a metropolitan county (101.7) and lowest in adjacent to metropolitan counties (74.1). In 2019-2020, less than 10% of all rural counties lacked a primary care physician (8.7%) or a nurse practitioner (5.7%), but about one in five rural counties (21.1%) did not have a physician assistant. Small and remote nonadjacent counties most frequently lacked a clinician.

There was also large variation in the supply of primary care clinicians across geographic regions of the U.S. The New England Census Division had more than twice the per capita supply of rural primary care physicians than the South Atlantic, East South Central, and West South Central Census Divisions. The New England Census Division had significantly higher ratios of nurse practitioners per 100,000 population than all other Census Divisions except the East South Central. In 2020, the East South Central Census Division had the highest per capita supply of nurse practitioners nationally.

<!-- image -->

## Behavioral Health Clinicians

From 2014 to 2021, the national supply of psychiatrists, psychologists, psychiatric nurse practitioners, social workers and counselors was significantly lower in rural counties compared to urban counties. Most recently, the number of behavioral clinicians per 100,000 population in rural counties ranged from 27% (psychiatrists) to 71% (psychiatric nurse practitioners) of that in urban counties. Micropolitan nonadjacent counties had the highest supply per 100,000 population among rural counties of psychiatrists (5.6), psychologists (21.7), psychiatric nurse practitioners (5.2), and social workers (80.2). Small and remote counties not adjacent to a metropolitan or micropolitan county had the highest per 100,000 population supply of counselors (132.4). In 2019-2021 many rural counties did not have a psychiatrist (70.2%), psychiatric nurse practitioner (68.5%), psychologist (45.3%), social worker (21.8%), or a counselor (18.4%). Small and remote counties regardless of their adjacency to larger counties most commonly did not have any behavioral health clinician.

There was also variation in the supply of behavioral health clinicians regionally across the U.S. The New England Census Division had the greatest rural supply of most types of behavioral health clinicians, with more than double the supply of psychiatrists (14.6), psychologists (68.9), and social workers (154.4) compared to all other Census Divisions. Rural New England had nearly double the supply of psychiatric nurse practitioners (9.3) compared to all other Census Divisions except for the rural East South Central (5.5). The East South Central had the second highest rural supply of psychiatric nurse practitioners, and the highest supply of nurse practitioners overall. The Pacific Census Division had the greatest supply of counselors compared to all other census divisions (236.1).

## Obstetrical Care Clinicians

Nationally, the supply of obstetricians and advanced practice midwives per 100,000 women of childbearing age was higher in urban areas than in rural areas. The ratio of obstetricians per 100,000 women of childbearing age in rural counties in 2019 was less than half the same ratio in urban counties (30.3 vs. 62.9, respectively). The ratio of advanced practiced midwives in rural counties (13.6) was about 85% of the urban ratio (16.1). The ratios of midwives (including midwives and lay midwives) per 100,000 women of childbearing age was higher in rural counties (5.1) compared to urban counties in 2023 (3.8). The number of advanced practice midwives grew substantially between 2014 and 2023, with increases of 61.9% in rural counties and 56.3% in urban counties. In 2019, the rural supply per 100,000 women of childbearing age of obstetricians was highest in micropolitan counties that were not adjacent to a metropolitan county (48.7). In 2023, the same rural areas had the highest supply of advanced practice midwives (18.1). The supply was lowest for both professions in small and remote rural counties that were adjacent to a micropolitan county (obstetricians 16.4, advanced practice midwives 12.4). The supply for midwives was highest in small and remote counties not adjacent to a metropolitan or micropolitan county (7.3) and was lowest in urban counties (3.8). Geographic variation in the supply of obstetrical services across U.S. Census Divisions was considerable. In rural counties, the supply of obstetricians and advanced practice midwives per 100,000 women of childbearing age was highest in the New England Census Division (61.2 and 51.1, respectively). The supply of advanced practice midwives in rural New England was more than twice that of all other Census Divisions, and the supply of obstetricians in rural New England was substantially higher than in all other Census Divisions. Rural counties much more frequently lacked any obstetrics service clinician than urban counties. In 2019, more than half of all U.S. rural counties lacked an obstetrician (57.4%), and in 2023 three-quarters lacked an advanced practice midwife (75.1%), and 87.4% lacked a midwife.

## General Surgeons

The per capita supply of general surgeons in rural areas in 2019 was about half of that in urban areas. The supply of general surgeons decreased in both rural (-11.8%) and urban (-8.4%) areas since 1995. The pattern of variation in the supply of general surgeons by type of rural area and U.S. Census Divisions was similar to that of other health care professionals. In 2019, the New England Census Division had the largest per 100,000 population supply in both rural (14.9) and urban counties (17.8)

<!-- image -->

while the West South Central had the lowest per 100,000 population supply in rural counties (4.6) and the West South Central and Mountain had the lowest supply in urban counties (11.3 and 11.0, respectively). Half of all U.S. rural counties (50.2%) in 2019 did not have a general surgeon compared to about one-fifth of urban counties (22.3%). Small and remote rural counties most frequently lacked a general surgeon.

## Dentists

Nationally in 2019, the ratio of dentists per 100,000 population in rural areas (32.1) was about 55% that of urban areas (59.4). From 1995 to 2019, the national rural per capita supply of dentists decreased by 7.0% while the urban ratio increased by 2.6%. There was significant variation in both the ratio of general dentists and specialist dentists per 100,000 population across U.S. Census Divisions. Among rural counties, the New England, Pacific and Mountain Census Divisions were in the top three Census Divisions for the per capita supply of both generalist and specialist dentists. In urban counties, the top three Census Divisions for generalist and specialist dentist supply per 100,000 population were the New England, Middle Atlantic, and Pacific Census Divisions. The dentist-to-population ratio also varied across diverse types of rural counties. Micropolitan counties not adjacent to a metropolitan county had the largest supply per 100,000 population in 2019 (42.8), and small and remote counties adjacent to a micropolitan county had the lowest supply (24.6). Almost four times as many rural counties lacked a dentist (14.0%) compared to urban counties (3.7%), and about one-quarter of all small and remote counties (regardless of adjacency) lacked a dentist.

## Community Health Workers

Community health workers (CHW) with an NPI are a growing part of the health care system. In 2021, there were about twice as many CHWs with an NPI per 100,000 population in rural counties as in urban areas (10.7 vs. 5.6, respectively). The populationadjusted supply of CHWs with an NPI has more than tripled in both rural and urban counties since 2014. However, there was significant supply variation across the U.S. In 2021, the Pacific Census Division had the highest rural supply per 100,000 population of CHWs with an NPI (48.3), while the Middle Atlantic Census Division had the lowest (1.4). Three-quarters of rural counties (77.0%) across the U.S. did not have a CHW with an NPI in 2021, compared to less than half (43.6%) of urban counties. U.S. states vary in the eligibility and requirements for CHWs to obtain an individual NPI number, and many states are currently in the process of implementing processes and requirements for reimbursement of CHW services. 48

## Home Health Aides

In 2021, the number of home health aides (HHA) per 100,000 population with an NPI in rural counties (2.8) was about one-third that of urban counties (8.0). The rural supply to population ratio increased about twice as much as in urban areas between 2014 and 2021 (180.0% vs. 95.1%, respectively). As with other health care professions, there was significant regional variation in the supply to population ratio of HHA with an NPI across U.S. Census Divisions. Rural counties in the East North Central Census Division had the largest HHA to population ratio (10.2 per 100,000), more than ten times the ratio of all other Census Divisions except New England and South Atlantic. More than three-quarters of rural counties (84.1%) did not have a HHA with an NPI in 2021, compared to less than half of urban counties (46.9%). Services provided by HHAs may be billed using a home health agency's NPI number which may explain the relatively small number of HHAs with an NPI.

## LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting results. First, the NPPES NPI data set was not designed as a research tool for tracking the health professional workforce but has been used as such due to limited availability of national health professional data. Because the NPPES NPI data are collected for administrative purposes, the methodology and frequency of removing clinicians from the dataset who are not practicing is not known. This may result in the inclusion of clinicians who are retired or not practicing in our analyses which could overestimate of the health workforce supply. This potential overestimation is mitigated by the fact that some health care clinicians do not bill directly for services or bill under a group NPI number, and therefore do not have an individual NPI number. These clinicians are not included in our workforce supply estimates, which results in an undercounting. In states with reduced or restricted practice regulations, some professions such as NPs and CHWs may be less likely to bill using their own NPI, which could account for some of the differences in regional supply shown here. 49  Our analyses of CHWs and home health workers are likely to have greatly undercounted these workforces as a whole and are best viewed as representing only the small proportions who bill CMS directly for their services.

<!-- image -->

Second, The NPPES NPI data do not provide a comprehensive set of specialty codes for all types of professions. This limits our ability to assign these professionals to content of care categories (eg, primary care, behavioral health). For example, there are only two taxonomy subcategories available for physician assistants (medical and surgical). Neither of these categories allows physician assistants who provide behavioral health care, for example, to self-designate themselves as a behavioral health care clinician. This prevents us from including them in our description of the behavioral health care workforce. This problem exists in the NPPES NPI data to differing degrees depending on the type of health care professional. Despite these limitations, the NPPES NPI remains one of the few nationally available data sources that includes practice location data that allows the level of rurality of the provider to be determined.

Finally, the data sources utilized in these analyses cannot be used to identify the geographic reach of health professionals who are providing services via telehealth. The significant change in clinical practice and reimbursement policies for telehealth services that were implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic changed how health care services are provided and make identifying areas where clinicians are providing services more complex. Many of these changes were made permanent in 2023 after the public health emergency ended, including eliminating the geographic restrictions where clinicians can be located for providing behavioral/mental telehealth services. 50  One study found that after the telehealth policy changes were made during the pandemic, more rural telehealth users received care from clinicians in another state than urban telehealth users. 51  Researchers will need to take telehealth usage into account and employ other approaches for assessing where rural patients access care.

## CONCLUSIONS

Disparities in the rural health workforce have persisted for decades. Despite explosive growth in some professions (eg, nurse practitioners and physician assistants), the population adjusted ratio of most rural clinician types continues to lag behind their urban counterparts. Rural communities report workforce recruitment and retention as one of their top challenges. 52  Data to document and track these disparities have not always been available for all types of healthcare workers. The county-level AHRF provides comprehensive and historic counts but does not have information about many key health care professions. Its focus includes physicians, dentists, and advanced practice clinicians, but it does not include information on nursing or allied health professions. While the NPI data include a much broader catchment of professions, those members of the health care team who do not have an individual NPI number are not captured, likely resulting in an undercount that may vary by profession. As a national health workforce database, the NPI's strengths include its broad profession coverage and the detailed (ZIP code) practice location information.

The cost for individual researchers or interested parties to collect and construct national databases is prohibitive. However, a nationally available dataset designed to track a comprehensive collection of health workforce professionals in both rural and urban places would be beneficial. Aggregated state-level licensing or credentialing data would be one way to do this. To assist rural communities in meeting the care requirements of their population, broader scopes of practice and emerging health care models, modalities, and professions will need to be taken into consideration. A national database designed for research and tracking purposes would need to take changes in health care professions into account to be useful for monitoring the health workforce trends in both rural and urban places.

## KEY FINDINGS

-    The number of physicians per 100,000 population in rural U.S. counties (120.0) was about 40% that of urban counties (302.0).

## Changes in the Supply and Rural-Urban Distribution of Psychiatrists in the U.S., 1995-2019

## KEY FINDINGS

- The number of psychiatrists per 100,000 population in rural U.S. counties (3.5) was about one fourth that of urban counties (13.0).
-  The ratio of psychiatrists to population decreased by 5.4% in rural counties and 11.6% in urban counties from 1995 to 2019.
-  The 2019 supply of psychiatrists per 100,000 population varied substantially across different types of rural places, from 5.6, in micro counties nonadjacent to metro counties, to 1.5, in small and remote adjacent to micro counties.
- Nearly three quarters (70.2%) of rural counties did not have a psychiatrist in 2019 compared to 27.1% of urban counties.

This report describes the rural-urban distribution of psychiatrists with an M.D. or D.O. degree (unless otherwise specified) in U.S. counties from 1995 to 2019. ## Changes in the Supply and Rural-Urban Distribution of Psychologists in the U.S., 2014-2021

## KEY FINDINGS

-   The number of psychologists per 100,000 population in rural U.S. counties (15.8) was less than half that of urban counties (39.5).

## Changes in the Supply and Rural-Urban Distribution of Counselors in the U.S., 2014-2021

## KEY FINDINGS

-   The number of counselors per 100,000 population in rural U.S. counties (87.7) was about two thirds that of urban counties (131.2).
-  The ratio of counselors to population increased in both rural and urban counties from 2014 to 2021, but the increase has been larger in urban counties (87.8% vs. 103.4%).
-  The 2021 supply of counselors per 100,000 population varied substantially across different types of rural places. Rural counties adjacent to metro counties had a ratio of 78.2, and small and remote counties adjacent to micro counties had a ratio of 65.9. These ratios were lower than those of nonadjacent micropolitan (116.4) and nonadjacent small and remote (132.4) counties.
- Nearly one fifth (18.4%) of rural counties did not have a counselor in 2021 compared to 4.6% of urban counties.

This report describes the rural-urban distribution of counselors in U.S. counties from 2014 to 2021. The following behavioral health and social service providers were included: counselors, addiction (substance use disorder) counselors, pastoral counselors, professional counselors, school counselors, marriage and family therapists, poetry therapists, psychoanalysts, behavioral analysts, and clinical neuropsychologists. Counties were categorized  using  the  U.S.  Department  of Agriculture Urban Influence Codes (UIC). For detailed county designations, see page 4.

## Changes in the Supply and Rural-Urban Distribution of Advanced Practice Midwives in the U.S., 2014-2023

## KEY FINDINGS

-    The number of advanced practice midwives per 100,000 women of childbearing age in rural U.S. counties (13.6) was about 85% of that in urban counties (16.1) in 2023.

-    The 2023 supply of advanced practice midwives per 100,000 women of childbearing age varied substantially across different types of rural places, from 18.1 in micro counties nonadjacent to metro counties, to 12.4 in both micro and small and remote counties adjacent to metro and small and remote counties adjacent to micro counties.

## Changes in the Supply and Rural-Urban Distribution of General Surgeons in the U.S., 1995-2019

## KEY FINDINGS

-  The number of surgeons per 100,000 population in rural U.S. counties (6.7) was about one-half that of urban counties (13.1).
- The ratio of surgeons to population decreased by 11.8% in rural counties and 8.4% in urban counties from 1995 to 2019.
-  The 2019 supply of surgeons per 100,000 population varied substantially across different types of rural places, from 10.6 in micro counties nonadjacent to metro counties, to 4.3 in small and remote adjacent to micro counties.
- About half (50.2%) of rural counties did not have a surgeon in 2019 compared to 22.3% of urban counties.

This  report  describes  the  rural-urban distribution of general surgeons with an MD or DO degree in U.S. counties from 1995 to 2019. Counties were categorized using the U.S. Department of Agriculture Urban Influence Codes (UIC). For detailed county designations, see page 4.

- Almost four times as many rural counties (14.0%) did not have a dentist in 2019 compared to urban counties (3.7%).

## Changes in the Supply and Rural-Urban Distribution of Community Health Workers in the U.S., 2014-2021

## KEY FINDINGS

-    The number of community health workers (CHWs) with National Provider Identifier (NPI) numbers per 100,000 population in rural U.S. counties (10.7) was almost twice that in urban counties (5.6).
- The ratio of CHWs with NPIs per 100,000 population more than tripled in both rural and urban counties from 2014 to 2021.
-  There was significant variation in the supply of CHWs with NPIs in rural areas across U.S. Census Divisions in 2021, ranging from 48.3 per 100,000 population in the Pacific to 1.4 per 100,000 population in the Middle Atlantic Census Divisions.
- Three-quarters of rural counties (77.0%) had no CHWs with NPIs in 2021 compared to 43.6% of urban counties.

This  report  describes  the  rural-urban distribution of community health workers (CHWs) with a National Provider Identifier (NPI) number, typically used by those who bill for their services, in U.S. counties from 2014 to 2021. Because CHWs without NPIs are not included, the estimates reported here do not represent the total population of CHWs. Counties were categorized using the U.S. Department of Agriculture Urban Influence Codes (UIC). For detailed county designations, see page 4.

## Changes in the Supply and Rural-Urban Distribution of Home Health Aides in the U.S., 2014-2021

## KEY FINDINGS

-    The number of home health aides (HHAs) with a National Provider Identifier (NPI) number per 100,000 population in rural U.S. counties (2.8) was about one-third that of urban counties (8.0).
- The ratio of HHAs with NPIs to population increased by 180.0% in rural counties and 95.1% in urban counties from 2014 to 2021.
-  The 2021 supply of HHAs with NPIs per 100,000 population varied substantially across different types of rural places, from 3.8 in micro counties adjacent to metro counties, to 0.8 in micro counties nonadjacent to metro counties.
-  More than three-quarters of rural counties (84.1%) did not have a HHA with an NPI in 2021 compared to less than half of urban counties (46.9%).

This  report  describes  the  rural-urban distribution of home health aides (HHAs) with a National Provider Identifier (NPI) in U.S. counties from 2014 to 2021. The only category of nursing service-related providers we included in these analyses was home health aide. Many services provided by HHAs are billed using the NPI of a home health agency. Counties were categorized using the U.S. Department of Agriculture Urban Influence Codes (UIC). For detailed county designations, see page 4.
