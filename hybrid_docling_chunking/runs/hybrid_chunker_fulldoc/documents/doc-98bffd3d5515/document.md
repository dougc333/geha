## Inequitable Access to Employer-Sponsored Benefits, Financial Stability, and Employee Turnover

Manita Rao and Jeremy Burke

## Abstract

As a provider of both wages and benefits, employers are uniquely positioned to influence employee financial stability. Inequities in benefit provision across demographic groups, however, may lead to systematic disparities in financial security.  This paper uses longitudinal data from a nationally representative internet panel to examine the post-pandemic evolution of racial/ethnic and sex disparities in access to employer-sponsored benefits and how benefits relate to employee financial stability.  We find substantial, persistent, and in some cases growing, disparities in access to benefits between Whites and non-Whites, and men and women, over the 2021 - 2023 period.  Importantly, these disparities matter - employer-sponsored benefits are systematically linked to higher employee financial security.  We also find that access to benefits is predictive of lower employee turnover, suggesting possible impacts on employer productivity. Our results suggest that persistent, inequitable access to employer-sponsored benefits may be contributing to the well-known gaps in financial security across race and sex.

## I. Introduction

Fringe benefits constitute a significant proportion of compensation workers receive in exchange for labor. According to the Department of Labor's National Compensation Survey, in 2022, wages and salaries cost employers $28.16 per hour and accounted for 68.8 percent of total compensation, while benefits cost $12.74 per hour and accounted for the remaining 31.2 percent of employer personnel costs. Over the past few decades, employers have sought to innovate on the benefits offered to workers and align benefit packages with workers' changing financial, family, and health needs.

Despite the importance of workplace benefits, employer-sponsored benefits are inequitably distributed. Benefit costs to employers, as a share of total compensation, vary considerably by household income. Employer benefits constitute a larger share of compensation costs for higher income workers than individuals at the lower end of the income distribution. 1 In 2022, employer benefit costs for private sector workers at the 90 th percentile of income was $74.46 per hour worked, 79 percent higher than employer benefit costs for workers at the 10 th percentile (Department of Labor, 2022).  Further, between 2013 and 2022, benefit costs for employees in the 90 th percentile of the income distribution rose by 35 percent, which is higher than the 32 percent increase for workers in the 10 th percentile.

This paper examines inequities in access to employer-sponsored benefits by demographic characteristics and their influence on employees' financial well -being and worker productivity. Using data from the Understanding America Study, a nationally representative internet panel of US households, the paper examines how disparities in access to employer-sponsored benefits evolved between 2021 and 2023 and assesses the impact of employer-sponsored benefits on several indicators of employee financial well-being and workplace productivity.

The findings reveal substantial, persistent, and in some cases growing, disparities in benefit access among certain demographic groups, as well as between men and women. Importantly, these disparities matter: employer-sponsored benefits are systematically linked to higher employee financial security.  The findings further reveal that access to benefits is predictive of lower employee turnover, suggesting possible impacts on employer productivity.

## II. The importance of employer-sponsored benefits in compensation

Employer-sponsored benefits have expanded over time. In addition to traditional benefits like health insurance and retirement savings, employers now often provide paid leave benefits such as sick leave, vacation leave, and personal leave. Employers have also expanded the types of insurance they provide to include life insurance and short-term and long-term disability insurance. More recently, employer-sponsored benefits have included emergency savings accounts and financial wellness benefits such as financial counseling and debt management advice.

Access to benefits vary by benefit type.  For instance, in 2022, medical care and health insurance benefits were available to 72 percent of private sector workers, but only 43 percent had access to dental benefits and 28 percent had access to vision benefits (Department of Labor, 2022). Access to benefits also vary widely by occupation. While 94 percent of workers in management, business, and financial occupations had access to medical care benefits, only 52 percent of workers in service occupations had access to these benefits. Similarly, only 56 percent of workers in establishments with less than 50 workers had access to medical care benefits; in establishments with 500 workers or more, access jumps to over 91 percent of workers.

Similarly, nearly 75 percent of private sector workers had access to retirement benefits in 2022, but access was inequitably distributed. Over 90 percent of workers in the management, business, and finance occupation had access to these benefits, compared to only 47 percent of workers in service occupations. Additionally, 93 percent of workers in the highest 10 th percentile of the wage distribution had access to retirement benefits, yet only 52 percent of workers in the bottom 25 th percentile had access to these benefits. Access to retirement benefits is also higher for employees of larger firms (90 percent) compared to smaller firms with less than 100 employees (58 percent). Similar trends are observed in the case of access to employer sponsored life insurance benefits. Nearly 83 percent of employees in management, business, and finance occupations had access to this benefit in 2022 compared to an overall access rate of 58 percent, indicating substantive disparities in which employers provide such benefits. And, while overall, 76 percent of employees had access to paid sick leave benefits, close to 96 percent of employees in management, business, and finance sectors had access to these benefits and between 64 to 66 percent of workers in retail services and construction industries had access to paid sick leave benefits.

Previous research shows that benefit access and employer costs for workplace benefits for certain occupations and income groups have outpaced others by significant margins. For instance, Bloomfield et. al. (2025) finds that between 1990 and 2020, rising non-wage costs are exacerbating compensation inequality by disproportionately benefiting higher wage workers. Despite well documented inequities in access to employer-sponsored benefits across occupations, less research has examined disparities in access by demographic characteristics. Moreover, an under-researched question is the extent to which benefit inequities influence workers' financial well -being. Further, there is relatively limited research on how provision of employer-sponsored benefits translates into productivity outcomes for employers.

## III. Data and sample characteristics

To examine the prevalence of, and disparities in, access to employer-sponsored benefits, this study draws on several years of data from the Understanding America Study (UAS).  The UAS is a probability-based internet panel that longitudinally tracks a U.S. representative sample of over 15,000 adults. Panel members are recruited exclusively through Address Based Sampling and receive a tablet and broadband access (and related training) if they do not have internet access. This mitigates selection problems facing convenience panels, where respondents are recruited from existing Internet users. The UAS contains a large set of background characteristics for all panel members, including demographic (e.g., age, gender, race, education), financial (e.g., income, financial literacy), health (e.g., self-assessed general health, selfreported doctor's diagnoses of conditions), personality traits (the big five) and cognition measures (e.g., number series, propositional analogies, picture vocabulary).

The primary analytic sample comes from three surveys fielded each year in April and May from 2021 to 2023.  The surveys were designed to measure respondents' financial stability in detail and contain numerous indicators of financial security and economic distress.  These measures include employment and income shocks, spending and saving behavior, debt accumulation and levels, and retirement savings behavior, among others. The paper leverages two key measures capturing overall financial situations.  The first asks respondents, ' Overall, how satisfied are you with your current financial situation?' on a 1 - 5 scale from 'extremely satisfied' to 'not at all satisfied . ' The second asks 'how much stress, if any, do your finances cause you?' with response options of 'high stress,' 'moderate stress,' 'some stress,' or 'no stress.' Responses are coded as high financial satisfaction if respondents report being either 'extremely satisfied' or 'very satisfied' with their financial situation. Responses are coded as financially stressed if respondents report that their financial situation is causing them either 'moderate' or 'high' stress. The paper also measures whether respondents are actively saving for emergencies by asking, ' A re you currently setting aside money for an emergency?' In 2023, the question was changed to elicit whether respondents are regularly setting aside money, setting aside money when they can but not regularly, or not setting aside money.  Responses are coded as saving for emergencies if respondents report currently setting aside money in 2021 or 2022 or regularly doing so in 2023. 2 Importantly for the research questions examined in this paper, these surveys also ask respondents whether they have access to a litany of employer-sponsored benefits programs, whether or not they personally use them.  In particular, the surveys elicit access to paid vacation or sick leave, health insurance, life insurance, disability insurance, defined contribution retirement plans, student loan repayment, access to an emergency savings account or contributions toward emergency savings, childcare, and an ability to work-from-home.  There were a few changes to the nature of the questions over time.  In the first two waves of the data, paid vacation and sick leave were asked about separately, yet elicited jointly in the 2023 wave. Accordingly, an indicator was created for whether a respondent had access to either paid vacation or sick leave in the 2021 and 2022 waves to preserve comparability.  Additionally, the 2021 and 2022 survey waves ask if respondents' employers provided 'childcare' benefits, while the 2023 survey asks whether employers prov ide 'subsidies to pay for childcare.' The paper assesses these questions as substantively similar and combine across waves.  Access to (or contributions towards) an emergency savings account was not asked in the 2021 wave.

## IV. Results: Disparities in Access

Sample averages can mask important differences in access across subgroups.  This section explores how access to employer-sponsored benefits varies across race/ethnicity and sex.  To do so, we divided the sample between non-Hispanic white adults and other groups in aggregate, and male and female.  When collapsing all such groups into a single category, we unfortunately lose insights into how experiences vary across different races and ethnicities.  While lamentable, we implement this categorization to preserve statistical power, as there are relatively few observations in most non-white racial categories.

Figure 1 explores differences in access to paid sick leave or paid vacation.  Over the full three years of the data, there are no statistically significant differences in access across nonHispanic white adults and the other groups in aggregate, on average.  In 2021 and 2022, those groups report statistically significantly higher levels of access, by 3 percentage points on average, yet this reverses in 2023 with non-Hispanic white adults reporting statistically significantly higher access (70% vs 65%).  As noted above, how access to these benefits was elicited changed in 2023 - combining separately asked questions about access to paid sick leave and access to paid vacation into a single question asking about access to either.  This change in elicitation method likely contributed to the reduced rate of reported access in 2023 observed across groups.

In contrast, women report consistently, and statistically significantly, lower rates of access throughout the period, though differences abate somewhat over time.  In 2021, men were

5 percentage points more likely to indicate they had access to employer-sponsored paid sick leave or paid vacation than women, yet this gap dropped to 2 percentage points in 2023.

The findings show little variation in access to student loan benefits or employer-sponsored emergency savings accounts (or contributions towards these accounts) across race/ethnicity and sex (not depicted).  In each of the three years, approximately 7% of all groups indicated that their employer offered access to student loan repayment.  Access to emergency savings accounts grew in the sample from approximately 2% in 2022 to 5% in 2023, with little observable differences between non-Hispanic white adults and other groups, or men and women, in either year.  We do observe racial and ethnic differences in access to childcare benefits - access among the aggregated groups was 2 percentage points higher than among non-Hispanic white adults in 2021 and 2022, yet statistically indistinguishable in 2023.  We do not observe any differences in access to childcare benefits by sex, on average, across our sample.

Taken together, the findings show sizeable, and in some cases growing, differences in access to commonly offered employer-sponsored benefits between non-Hispanic White individuals and those in other groups, with the former consistently reporting higher levels of access to benefits.  We also find that men have persistently higher levels of access to employersponsored benefits than women, with some gaps between the sexes closing over time.  Part of the sex differences are attributable to differences in the nature of employment across men and women, who are approximately twice as likely to be working part-time than men in our sample

(22% vs 11%); benefits access is systematically lower among part-time employees.  Still, this difference does not fully explain gaps in access between men and women.  Restricting the sample to only those who are working full-time, the findings still show statistically significant gaps in access to retirement accounts and disability insurance across sex even though  NonHispanic white and workers of all other groups are equally likely to be working full-time in the sample.

Table 4 examines the relationship between employer-sponsored benefits and financial satisfaction.  As above, we denote individuals as having high financial satisfaction if they report being 'extremely' or 'very' satisfied with their financial situation. We find that access to employer-sponsored life insurance, disability insurance, and an ability to work-from-home are all statistically significantly, linked with high levels of financial satisfaction. For example, individuals who work for employers that provide life insurance benefits are 3.3 percentage points more likely to be highly financially satisfied, a 10 percent increase relative to the sample mean. The findings show similar estimated effect sizes for two less commonly offered benefits - student loan repayment and childcare benefits - though the estimates are imprecise given low incidence of these benefits in our sample.  Importantly, all of the point estimates are positive, suggesting each benefit is at least directionally related to higher financial security.

Table 5 examines associations between employer-sponsored benefits and financial stress. Paid vacation or sick leave, access to a defined contribution plan, and access to health, life, and disability insurance are all statistically significantly associated with a lower likelihood of being financially stressed.  For example, those working for an employer who provides paid leave are 3.7 percentage points less likely to report 'high' or 'moderate' amounts of stress due to their financial situation (an 11% reduction relative to the sample mean).  There are also large, estimated effects for access to an emergency savings account and childcare benefits, though neither estimate is statistically significant given a lack of statistical precision due to these benefits being relatively scarce in the sample.  Similar to the analysis for financial satisfaction, the findings show that all the employer-sponsored benefits studied are at least directionally positively related to financial security - in this case negatively related to financial stress.

Table 6 explores how employer-sponsored benefits relate to emergency savings behavior. The findings show a clear relationship - employer-sponsored benefits are associated with a higher likelihood of saving for emergencies.  Paid vacation or sick leave, access to a defined contribution plan, an ability to work from home, student loan repayment benefits, childcare benefits and access to health, life, and disability insurance are all significantly associated with saving for emergencies.  For example, an ability to work from home is associated with a 5.6 percentage point increase in the likelihood of actively saving for emergencies (an 11 percent increase relative to the sample mean).  Thus, our results are consistent with the possibility that employer-sponsored benefits help facilitate savings behavior.  Working from home, for example, may reduce employees' transportation costs, increasing the likelihood they devote those resources towards savings.  While encouraging for employees who work at firms that offer such benefits, it is also somewhat concerning for those who do not.  The results imply that individuals who do not have access to employer-sponsored health insurance, for example, are also less likely to be saving for emergencies, potentially leaving them both at risk for higher surprise medical bills and increased difficulty paying for them.

These findings underscore that the racial/ethnic and sex disparities in access to employersponsored benefits that are observed matter - access to employer-sponsored benefits are systematically linked to higher financial security.  These findings suggest that inequitable access to employer-sponsored benefits may be contributing to the well-known gaps in financial security across race and sex (Hasler et al., 2018, Lusardi et al., 2025).

## VII. Conclusion

Employers are uniquely positioned to help their employees secure financial stability.  Beyond wages, employers also frequently provide benefits, such as paid time off and health insurance, which can play an important role in helping individuals cope with life shocks and financial associated with hiring and training new workers.

This paper uses longitudinal data from a nationally representative internet panel to examine the evolution of racial and gender gaps in access to employer-sponsored benefits over the 2021 to 2023 period and explore how benefits relate to financial security.  We find Hispanic white adults and those in other groups.  For example, in 2021 working non-Hispanic White adults were 2 percentage points more likely than working members of other groups to have access to employer-sponsored health insurance, yet this gap more than doubled to 5 percentage points in 2023.  The findings also show that men have persistently higher levels of access to benefits than women, with gaps in access to some benefits narrowing during our window of analysis.

Importantly, the analyses of the relationship between employer-sponsored benefits and employee financial security underscore that these racial/ethnic and sex disparities matter.  The finding show access to employer-sponsored benefits is systematically linked to higher financial security.  For example, access to a defined contribution plan, health, life, and disability insurance (benefits with notable racial/ethnic and sex gaps in access) are all statistically significantly associated with a lower likelihood of being financially stressed.   Moreover, there is consistent evidence that access to employer-sponsored benefits is positively related to saving for emergencies. This suggests that employer benefit provision may help facilitate emergency saving behavior, potentially further mitigating the impacts of incurred life shocks.

The findings also present evidence suggesting that benefit provision can directly help employers; all the benefits examined (except for employer-sponsored emergency savings accounts) are at least directionally associated with lower employee turnover.  There are statistically significant associations with two benefits, paid leave and an ability to work from home, suggesting that provision of these benefits reduce annual turnover on the order of 25%.

These results suggest that persistent, inequitable access to employer-sponsored benefits may be contributing to the well-known gaps in financial security across race and sex (Hasler et al., 2018, Lusardi et al., 2025).  Additionally, the finding reveal that increased provision of benefits might not only reduce disparities in financial stability but may also improve employer outcomes by reducing employee turnover. It is important to note, however, that while the analyses find consistent results across a host of benefits, they do not establish causality. Additionally, due to data limitations, the paper is not able to conduct a cost-benefit analysis from the employer's perspective.  These important issues await future work.
